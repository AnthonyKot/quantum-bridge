Quantum mechanics and quantum information

The Bridge

Learn quantum information by working through the calculations: measurement probabilities, gate sequences, communication protocols, algorithms, and error correction.

These fifteen chapters assume complex numbers and linear algebra. They introduce the quantum-information notation as it is needed. You can read in sequence or use the prerequisite notes to enter at a later chapter.

Start with qubits and relative phase →

Each chapter includes derivations and two problems with expandable worked answers. Try the problems before opening the answers; being able to reproduce a calculation is a better checkpoint than recognising its final formula.

States and measurements

Calculate observable probabilities before introducing circuits.

  1. 01 Qubits, amplitudes, and relative phaseHow can two preparations give identical measurement statistics and still be different states? A second measurement reveals the relative phase between a qubit's amplitudes.
  2. 02 Measurement: probabilities and state changesA qubit reads 0 with probability ¾. What state is it in afterwards, and do the probabilities decide that? Two apparatuses with identical statistics leave different states.
  3. 03 Density matrices and the state of a subsystemCan any measurement tell a coin flip between |0⟩ and |1⟩ from a coin flip between |+⟩ and |−⟩? One matrix holds every prediction, for mixtures and for half of an entangled pair.

Gates and control

Follow matrix products and derive rotations from a Hamiltonian.

  1. 04 Gates, circuits, and universalityWhat does each gate do to the amplitudes, and what can a controlled gate change that one-qubit gates cannot? Two gates followed from |00⟩ to a Bell pair.
  2. 05 Rotations and physical control of a qubitFor how long, and about which axis, must a qubit be driven to reach a chosen measurement probability? From a Hamiltonian to a rotation of the Bloch vector.

Entanglement and communication

Establish what can be copied, correlated, and transmitted.

  1. 06 Why an unknown state cannot be copiedA machine copies |0⟩ and |1⟩. What does it do to |+⟩, and could any machine copy every state? Linearity forces entanglement instead of copies.
  2. 07 Entanglement and the Schmidt decompositionGiven a two-qubit state’s amplitudes, is it a product, and if not, how far from one? The coefficient matrix and its singular values answer both.
  3. 08 Bell correlations and the CHSH inequalityFixed instructions from the source cap a combination of correlations at 2; a Bell pair gives 2√2. What that rules out, and what it leaves standing.
  4. 09 Teleportation and dense coding, step by stepWhat must Alice send so Bob recovers an unknown qubit, and why can he not have it sooner? Every register tracked, plus the reverse protocol.

Quantum algorithms

Track phases, sampling distributions, and query costs.

  1. 10 Phase kickback and the Deutsch–Jozsa algorithmA function value can change a phase without leaving a readable value in the data register. A final change of basis converts a particular pattern of phases into an answer.
  2. 11 Fourier sampling, phase estimation, and periodsThe quantum Fourier transform changes amplitudes into Fourier amplitudes. Its output becomes useful when the sampling distribution reveals a phase or a period.
  3. 12 Grover search as two reflectionsHow can a sign flip, followed by a second operation, make an answer more likely, and why must we stop repeating that pair? Two reflections make a rotation that approaches the marked item and then passes it.

Noise and information

Model open evolution, construct recovery, and count message information.

  1. 13 Quantum channels: discarded environments and noiseWhen a system interacts with an environment, its reduced evolution need not be unitary. The channel formalism calculates what remains accessible.
  2. 14 Error correction without reading the logical stateAn error-correcting code stores a qubit in a subspace. Measuring parity can identify a recoverable error while preserving the amplitudes inside that subspace.
  3. 15 Entropy, accessible information, and the Holevo boundA state can have large entropy while carrying no information about a message. To quantify communication, compare the average state with the states assigned to individual messages.
