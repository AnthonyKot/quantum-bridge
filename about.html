The method

About this book

One shelf holds a physics course; another holds a computing book. The claim of The Bridge is that they are the same shelf, walked in a different order.

One essay tests that claim rather than repeating it. Essay 08 (EPR and Bell) is the place where “no new physics” is arguably false — Bell's theorem is a genuinely new empirical prediction — and the book confronts it rather than hiding it, refining the thesis instead of defending it: quantum information added no new postulates, but asking information-theoretic questions of the old ones can produce new physics.

The two ends of every crossing

The course side is two texts read together. Landau & Lifshitz, Quantum Mechanics (Vol. 3 of the Course of Theoretical Physics) is the anchor — the same tradition featured in the sibling collection, The Quantum Quartet — chosen because it builds the subject from the superposition principle and the algebra of operators, so its scaffolding already looks like the linear algebra of quantum information. But L&L is also the most austere course text in the canon: it has no two-level-system tensor products, no notion of information, and no Bell inequality. Where it stops short, the essays bring in Sakurai, Modern Quantum Mechanics, whose opening chapters — Stern–Gerlach, two-state systems, spin — are very nearly qubits already, and which treats EPR and Bell explicitly. The two are cited side by side, whichever mirrors a given result more cleanly.

And where neither course reaches the information-theoretic idea — entanglement as a usable resource, say — the essay says so in as many words, rather than pretending the crossing was there all along. Naming the gap is part of the method, not an admission against it: half the point of the book is to show exactly how far the standard curriculum carries you and where the genuinely new step is taken.

The result side is Nielsen & Chuang, Quantum Computation and Quantum Information (the 10th-anniversary edition). Each essay ends on a specific, named object from that book — a theorem, a protocol, an algorithm — and cites the section where it lives.

The four moves

Every essay has the same shape, so the crossing is always visible:

  1. In the course. The topic as a physics student meets it, in L&L's (or Sakurai's) own framing, with section pointers.
  2. The step across. The single reinterpretation that turns a statement about physics into a statement about information. This is where the essay earns its title.
  3. The result. The Nielsen & Chuang object, stated precisely, with a short worked example so it is concrete rather than quoted.
  4. Reading. Where to find both ends — L&L § and N&C § — to check the crossing for yourself.

Level and notation

The intended reader has had, or is having, a first serious quantum-mechanics course: comfortable with Hilbert spaces, Hermitian operators, and Dirac notation, but not assumed to have seen any quantum computing. Mathematics is written out — displayed equations render through KaTeX — but every step is motivated. Where L&L and N&C use different notation for the same object (L&L's $\hat{H}$ and N&C's gate matrices, for instance), the essay says so explicitly.

On sources

This book reproduces no copyrighted text from either work. It cites section numbers and states results in original prose and original examples, in the same spirit as its sibling collection. Readers are pointed toward legitimately available copies of the books; the value here is the crossing between them, not a substitute for either. Section pointers are given only at a granularity that can be stood behind — and because Sakurai's numbering shifts between editions, its crossings are cited by chapter and topic rather than by a section number that might not match your copy.

For the record, the reference editions: N&C is the 10th Anniversary Edition (2010), and its pointers have been checked against the text — §1.3.5 (“Qubit copying circuit?”), §1.3.7 (teleportation), §2.3 (superdense coding), §2.6 (“EPR and the Bell inequality”) are verified. Sakurai is the 3rd edition (Napolitano); its EPR/Bell material is §3.10, cited topic-first with the section in parentheses. L&L is a scan of the 3rd edition; its Contents was OCR'd to verify the section numbers used here — §2 (the principle of superposition), §7 (the wave function and measurements), §8 (the Hamiltonian operator), §14 (the density matrix), Ch VIII (Spin, §54–60) and Ch IX (Identity of particles, §61–65) are all confirmed against the book.

Status

The scaffold and the full fifteen-essay map are in place, and fourteen of the fifteen essays are written in full. Parts I (states), II (dynamics), III (entanglement), and V (open systems & information) are complete; Part IV — interference and algorithms — wants only its search essay (12), mapped on the contents page, the authoritative list of what is written.
