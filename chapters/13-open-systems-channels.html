Part V · Open systems & information — Essay 13

Open systems, and everything that can happen to a state

The course computes one decay — an excited atom emitting into a field it writes down. Nielsen & Chuang call that the amplitude-damping channel and ask a question the course never poses: what is the most general thing that can happen to a state? On the way it settles a debt essay 02 left in print.

The resultN&C Ch 8 — quantum operations, Kraus operators, the amplitude-damping channel

Move 1 In the course

Put an excited atom in the electromagnetic field and the course knows what to do. The coupling to the field is a time-dependent perturbation; Fermi's golden rule gives the rate of transitions into the continuum of field modes (L&L's time-dependent perturbation theory, §40–43, with the transition rate into a continuum in §43); and the excited-state population drains away exponentially,

$$ \rho_{11}(t) = \rho_{11}(0)\,e^{-t/T_1}, $$

with $T_1$ the lifetime. Two things about how the calculation is set up matter for what follows. The environment is written down explicitly — the field is in the Hamiltonian, atom and field evolve together by a perfectly ordinary unitary, and you obtain the atom's fate by keeping track of that whole closed system. And the calculation answers one question: what does this process do? The course computes a particular decay, for a particular coupling. It never asks what the entire menu of possible fates might be.

Move 2 The step across

Two moves turn that decay into the object N&C study. The first is to abstract the environment. Rather than write the field's Hamiltonian, treat the environment as an inaccessible ancilla in a fixed state, couple system and ancilla by some joint unitary $U$, and — as in Essays 02 and 03 — trace the ancilla out. What the system undergoes is then no longer a unitary but a quantum operation, and it always takes the operator-sum form

$$ \mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_k A_k\,\rho\,A_k^\dagger, \qquad \sum_k A_k^\dagger A_k = I, $$

whose $A_k$ are the Kraus operators. The second move is to ask the question the course skips. Not “what does this environment do,” but “what is the most general transformation a quantum state can suffer?” The answer is the completely-positive, trace-preserving (CPTP) maps, and every one of them has a Kraus form — equivalently (the Stinespring picture), every one is a unitary on the system plus an ancilla, followed by discarding the ancilla. We take that as a fact and show the construction rather than prove it, exactly as Essay 02 did with Naimark: the two are the same statement, once for measurements and once for dynamics. This question has no course-side counterpart at all. The course always keeps the environment in view and computes cases; it never maps the space those cases live in.

And here the debt from Essay 02 comes due. There, a general measurement had operators $\{A_m\}$: outcome $m$ arrived with probability $\langle\psi|A_m^\dagger A_m|\psi\rangle$, leaving the state proportional to $A_m|\psi\rangle$, and the POVM element was

$$ E_m = A_m^\dagger A_m. $$

A channel is the very same operators with the outcome forgotten — summed over instead of selected among. Measurement keeps the label $m$ and the state it left behind; the channel throws both away and averages. So a POVM is a quantum operation with the post-measurement state discarded, and $E_m = A_m^\dagger A_m$ is the exact bridge between them: one set of operators, read as a measurement if you record which one fired, as noise if you do not.

Move 3 The result

Nielsen & Chuang develop this as Chapter 8, and the decay of Move 1 is their headline example, amplitude damping (§8.3.5). It is a channel on a single qubit with just two Kraus operators,

$$ A_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sqrt{1-\gamma} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sqrt{\gamma} \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \gamma = 1 - e^{-t/T_1}, $$

and they resolve the identity: $A_0^\dagger A_0 = \mathrm{diag}(1,\,1-\gamma)$ and $A_1^\dagger A_1 = \mathrm{diag}(0,\,\gamma)$ sum to $I$. Read what each does. $A_1 = \sqrt{\gamma}\,|0\rangle\langle 1|$ is the branch where the atom emits its quantum and drops from $|1\rangle$ to $|0\rangle$; $A_0$ leaves the ground state alone and shrinks the excited amplitude by $\sqrt{1-\gamma}$ — the no-emission branch, which still reshapes the state. Apply the channel to the excited population and

$$ \rho_{11} \;\longmapsto\; (1-\gamma)\,\rho_{11} \;=\; e^{-t/T_1}\,\rho_{11}, $$

the exponential decay of Move 1 exactly, with the course's lifetime $T_1$ now sitting inside the damping parameter $\gamma$. The physics is identical; what changed is that the field is gone from the description, abstracted into an ancilla nobody reads.

That abstraction is the point of the enlargement. Amplitude damping is one point in the space of all channels, and the course computes that point — as spontaneous emission — without ever drawing the space. Nielsen & Chuang draw the space: every CPTP map, indexed by its Kraus operators. It is the same widening that Essay 04 performs for dynamics — from one Hamiltonian's evolution to the whole reachable unitary group — and that Essay 15 performs for readout, from one measurement's yield to all the information a state can surrender. The course solves the instances; quantum information characterizes the space they inhabit.

The debt-repayment doubles as a physical picture. Put a photodetector on the field and you are measuring which Kraus branch occurred — a click is outcome $A_1$, silence is $A_0$ — a POVM with $E_0 = A_0^\dagger A_0$, $E_1 = A_1^\dagger A_1$, the measurement of Essay 02. Leave the emitted photon unwatched and you must sum the branches: the same operators become the amplitude-damping channel. Whether spontaneous emission is a measurement or noise is nothing but whether someone is looking at the light.

Move 4 Reading

Course side. Landau & Lifshitz, time-dependent perturbation theory, §40–43 — the golden rule appears as the transition rate into a continuum in §43, and exponential decay with a lifetime $T_1$ is its consequence. The environment is modelled explicitly and one process is computed; that is the whole difference from what follows.

Result side. Nielsen & Chuang, Chapter 8: §8.2 quantum operations, §8.2.3 the operator-sum (Kraus) representation, §8.2.4 the axiomatic characterization of CPTP maps, and §8.3.5 amplitude damping. Scope, on purpose: this is the single-step channel picture. Continuous-time master equations of Lindblad type are a genuinely different subject and are left out; letting them in would swallow the essay.

How it connects. This pays the promissory note of Essay 02 — $E_m = A_m^\dagger A_m$, the Kraus channel being a POVM with the post-measurement states kept — and it reuses the trace-out of Essay 03, now producing dynamics rather than a static mixed state. The von Neumann entropy and Holevo bound of Essay 15 will ask how much a channel like this one can still carry.

Check the crossing. Confirm $A_0^\dagger A_0 + A_1^\dagger A_1 = I$ for amplitude damping, then apply $\mathcal{E}$ to a general $\rho$ and read off $\rho_{11}\mapsto(1-\gamma)\rho_{11}$, recovering $e^{-t/T_1}$. Finally check that $E_0=A_0^\dagger A_0$ and $E_1=A_1^\dagger A_1$ are a valid POVM — the measurement you get by watching for the photon.
