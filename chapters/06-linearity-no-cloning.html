Linearity & the no-cloning theorem

A physics course spends a term insisting that quantum evolution is linear. It never mentions that this one word forbids a photocopier. Nielsen & Chuang give the ban a name.

→

Move 1 In the course

Two facts from the opening chapters of Landau & Lifshitz do all the work here, and a course states both without ceremony. The first is that closed-system evolution is generated by the Schrödinger equation,

$$ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial \psi}{\partial t} = \hat{H}\psi, $$

which is linear in $\psi$ (L&L §8). The second is the superposition principle of §2: superpositions are legitimate states. Put together, they say that if the state $\psi_1$ evolves over some fixed interval to $\Phi_1$, and $\psi_2$ evolves to $\Phi_2$, then

$$ c_1\psi_1 + c_2\psi_2 \ \longrightarrow\ c_1\Phi_1 + c_2\Phi_2. $$

The machine that carries out this evolution — the time-evolution operator $\hat{U} = e^{-i\hat H t/\hbar}$ — is unitary and linear. It cannot know whether the vector fed to it is a "pure" basis state or a superposition; it acts on the vector, term by term. This is not a subtle point in the course. It is the point. Every legal quantum operation is a linear map on the state space.

Move 2 The step across

Now ask an engineer's question that a physics course never poses: can we build a device that copies an unknown quantum state? Classically this is trivial — reading a bit and writing it again is the basis of all computing. State it precisely for quantum systems. A copier would be a fixed unitary $\hat U$ acting on the data register plus a "blank" register prepared in some standard state $|b\rangle$, such that for every input state $|\psi\rangle$,

$$ \hat U\big(|\psi\rangle \otimes |b\rangle\big) = |\psi\rangle \otimes |\psi\rangle. $$

The word "every" is the whole crossing. We are not asking to copy one known state — that is easy, just prepare a fresh one. We are asking for a single device that works on any input, including inputs we have never seen and cannot measure without disturbing. The demand is exactly that a linear machine reproduce an arbitrary vector. Move 1 already told us linear machines distribute over superposition. We only have to feed the copier a superposition and watch the two requirements collide.

Move 3 The result

The collision is the no-cloning theorem (Wootters & Zurek, and Dieks, 1982), which Nielsen & Chuang prove in §1.3.5 — the section pointedly titled “Qubit copying circuit?”. The proof is four lines and uses nothing but linearity.

The proof

Suppose such a $\hat U$ exists. In particular it copies the two basis states of a qubit:

$$ \hat U\big(|0\rangle|b\rangle\big) = |0\rangle|0\rangle, \qquad \hat U\big(|1\rangle|b\rangle\big) = |1\rangle|1\rangle. $$

Consider the equal superposition $|{+}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$. Linearity (Move 1) dictates the output — the machine cannot do anything but act on each term:

$$ \hat U\big(|{+}\rangle|b\rangle\big) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big(\hat U|0\rangle|b\rangle + \hat U|1\rangle|b\rangle\Big) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle|0\rangle + |1\rangle|1\rangle\big). $$

But the definition of cloning demands the output be $|{+}\rangle|{+}\rangle$ — and that is not merely a vector with different numbers in it, but a different kind of vector. What linearity produced,

$$ \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|00\rangle + |11\rangle\big) = |\beta_{00}\rangle, $$

is a Bell state: it is entangled, provably impossible to write as any product $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ of two single-qubit states. Cloning, by contrast, demands the manifest product $|{+}\rangle\otimes|{+}\rangle$. No choice of amplitudes can turn a product into an entangled state — being entangled is a structural property of the vector, not a matter of coefficients to be tuned — so the two requirements cannot both hold. A single linear machine cannot clone. No universal quantum copier exists. $\blacksquare$

That is the sharper way to see it, and it is worth saying in one sentence: fed a superposition, a linear machine entangles the copy with the original instead of duplicating it. Cloning fails precisely because entanglement (Essay 07) succeeds.

The one-line version

The same fact falls out of inner products, which is the form N&C emphasize. If $\hat U$ cloned two states $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$, unitarity preserves their overlap, so taking the inner product of $\hat U|\psi\rangle|b\rangle$ with $\hat U|\phi\rangle|b\rangle$ gives

$$ \langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \big(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\big)^2. $$

The only solutions are $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = 0$ or $1$: a copier can work only on a set of mutually orthogonal states — that is, on states that are already, in effect, classical labels. The moment two inputs genuinely overlap, as $|0\rangle$ and $|{+}\rangle$ do, cloning is impossible. This is exactly why the theorem is a statement about information and not just about spins: orthogonal states are distinguishable, non-orthogonal states are not, and only the distinguishable ones can be copied.

Why it was proved: a wall against faster-than-light signalling

No-cloning did not arrive as an abstract meditation on copiers. It was written to kill a specific attempt to break relativity. In 1981 Nick Herbert circulated a proposal he called FLASH — First Laser-Amplified Superluminal Hookup — for signalling faster than light. Take one half of an entangled pair; a distant experimenter chooses a measurement basis, collapsing your half into some polarization. You cannot tell which basis they chose from a single photon — but, Herbert argued, run it through a laser gain tube and amplify it into many copies, and the statistics of the copies would reveal the basis, carrying one bit instantly across any distance.

The referees, Asher Peres among them, could not immediately name the flaw — and that pressure is what produced the theorem. Wootters & Zurek, and independently Dieks, showed the amplifier Herbert needed is exactly a universal cloner, and the four lines above forbid it. The gain tube must add noise; the copies are useless; FLASH fails. So the very first role of no-cloning in physics was custodial: it is the reason entanglement, for all its nonlocal correlation, cannot transmit information faster than light. Read this way, the linearity of the Schrödinger equation is not a mathematical convenience — it is what protects causality. The same lesson returns as the mandatory classical phone call in teleportation (Essay 09): quantum correlations never signal on their own.

Why it matters downstream

No-cloning is the load-bearing wall of quantum information. It is why an eavesdropper cannot silently duplicate a key in quantum cryptography; why quantum error correction has to protect states without ever reading or copying them (Essay 14); and why teleportation (Essay 09) necessarily destroys the original as it recreates it elsewhere — a faithful move, never a copy. Each of those results is downstream of the four-line argument above, which in turn is downstream of a single word from the physics course: linear.

Move 4 Reading

Course side. Landau & Lifshitz, Quantum Mechanics (Vol. 3): §2 (superposition) and §8 (the Hamiltonian and the time-dependent equation $i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi = \hat H\psi$), whose linearity is the entire hypothesis. The same linearity is explicit in Sakurai's construction of the time-evolution operator $\hat U = e^{-i\hat H t/\hbar}$ (Modern Quantum Mechanics, the time-evolution chapter). No gap here — linearity is stated plainly in every course; the surprise is only what it costs.

Result side. Nielsen & Chuang, §1.3.5 (“Qubit copying circuit?”) for the statement and proof; the information-theoretic reading — that only distinguishable, i.e. orthogonal, states can be copied — is the theme of §12.1, “Distinguishing quantum states and the accessible information.” The original papers are Wootters & Zurek, Nature 299 (1982) 802, and Dieks, Phys. Lett. A 92 (1982) 271.
