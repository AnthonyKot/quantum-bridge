Chapter 06

Why an unknown state cannot be copied

Copying two orthogonal basis states is possible. Requiring the same apparatus to copy their superpositions leads to a contradiction.

Specify what copying would mean

A copier receives a system in a state $|\psi\rangle$ and a blank register in a fixed state $|b\rangle$. It must produce $|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$ for every allowed input without being told which input it received. The words “same apparatus” matter: if a classical description of $|\psi\rangle$ is supplied, a preparation procedure can simply be run twice.

CNOT already copies a restricted set: $|0\rangle|0\rangle\mapsto|00\rangle$ and $|1\rangle|0\rangle\mapsto|11\rangle$. Apply that operation to a superposition:

$$\operatorname{CNOT}\left[(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)|0\rangle\right]=\alpha|00\rangle+\beta|11\rangle.$$

Two independent copies would instead be

$$|\psi\rangle^{\otimes2}=\alpha^2|00\rangle+\alpha\beta|01\rangle+\alpha\beta|10\rangle+\beta^2|11\rangle.$$

The amplitudes of a physical linear map depend linearly on the input amplitudes; the desired expression is quadratic. For $|+\rangle$, CNOT produces a Bell pair, not $|++\rangle$. The Bell pair has only $00$ and $11$ outcomes in the computational basis. Independent copies produce all four outcomes equally.

The inner-product proof

The overlap proof uses one property of composite systems that is worth writing out. If the two registers are independent product states, their inner product factorises:

$$(\langle a|\otimes\langle b|)(|c\rangle\otimes|d\rangle)=\langle a|c\rangle\,\langle b|d\rangle.$$

To derive it, expand each local state in an orthonormal basis. The joint inner product is $\sum_{i,j}a_i^*b_j^*c_id_j$. The $i$ and $j$ sums are independent, so it equals $(\sum_i a_i^*c_i)(\sum_jb_j^*d_j)$. We will apply this rule to the proposed product outputs, not to an arbitrary entangled output.

Next, unitarity gives $\langle Uv|Uw\rangle=\langle v|U^\dagger U|w\rangle=\langle v|w\rangle$. Thus a unitary cannot change the overlap of two possible inputs. The blank adds no distinguishability because it is the same normalised state in both cases: its overlap with itself is one. Two copies would multiply the input overlap by itself. For an overlap strictly between zero and one in magnitude, squaring makes it smaller, contradicting preservation by the unitary.

Suppose one unitary copies two states $|\psi\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle$, with the same normalised blank. Unitarity preserves their input overlap. Writing $s=\langle\psi|\phi\rangle$ gives

$$\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\langle b|b\rangle=\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|\phi\rangle,\qquad s=s^2.$$

For fixed vector representatives this requires $s=0$ or $s=1$. Allowing physically irrelevant output phases gives the phase-independent conclusion $|s|=|s|^2$: the input physical states must be orthogonal or identical. A ray is the set of nonzero vectors that differ only by an overall scalar; after normalisation, its representatives differ only by a common phase. Quantum pure states are rays in this sense. Distinct nonorthogonal states cannot both be copied exactly by that unitary. Since a universal copier would have to copy $|0\rangle$ and $|+\rangle$, whose overlap magnitude is $1/\sqrt2$, it cannot exist.

An extra apparatus does not evade the result

Allow the copier to retain a record, $U|\psi,b,a\rangle=|\psi,\psi\rangle|a_\psi\rangle$. The two output copies are pure, so any pure global output with those exact copies must factor from the remaining apparatus. Preservation of overlap now requires

$$s=s^2\langle a_\psi|a_\phi\rangle.$$

For $0<|s|<1$, taking magnitudes would require $|\langle a_\psi|a_\phi\rangle|=1/|s|>1$, which is impossible for normalised apparatus states. General deterministic physical channels can be represented by such a unitary with an environment and a discarded record. Thus adding an environment or forgetting an outcome does not create a deterministic exact copier.

Why measuring first does not solve the problem

One might try to identify the input and then prepare two copies. Perfect discrimination of nonorthogonal states is itself impossible. For a short proof, let effect $E$ identify $|\psi\rangle$ with certainty and never occur on $|\phi\rangle$. Here $0\le E\le I$ means that both $E$ and $I-E$ are positive semidefinite: every expectation $\langle\chi|E|\chi\rangle$ lies between $0$ and $1$. The assumptions are $\langle\psi|E|\psi\rangle=1$ and $\langle\phi|E|\phi\rangle=0$, with $0\le E\le I$.

Positivity implies $(I-E)|\psi\rangle=0$: a positive semidefinite operator $P$ with $\langle\psi|P|\psi\rangle=0$ satisfies $\|\sqrt P|\psi\rangle\|^2=0$, hence $\sqrt P|\psi\rangle=0$ and $P|\psi\rangle=0$ and $E|\phi\rangle=0$. Therefore $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle=\langle\psi|E|\phi\rangle=0$. Certainty for one and impossibility for the other requires orthogonality. Chapter 2's discrimination scheme avoids this obstruction by explicitly allowing an inconclusive outcome.

The boundaries of the theorem

No-cloning forbids deterministic, exact copying of arbitrary unknown states. It allows copying a known orthogonal alphabet, preparing a known state repeatedly, approximate copying with imperfect outputs, and some probabilistic procedures with reported failure. It does not forbid transferring a state: teleportation consumes the original encoding and requires classical communication.

It also does not forbid encoding one qubit into many physical qubits. The map $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\mapsto\alpha|000\rangle+\beta|111\rangle$ is linear. Its output stores amplitudes jointly; the individual qubits do not each contain $|\psi\rangle$. Chapter 14 uses precisely this distinction.

Problems

1. For the CNOT output $\alpha|00\rangle+\beta|11\rangle$, find the state of the second qubit. When does it equal the original pure input?

Worked answer

Form the projector of the output $|\Psi\rangle=\alpha|00\rangle+\beta|11\rangle$. Its four terms have coefficients $|\alpha|^2,\alpha\beta^*,\beta\alpha^*,|\beta|^2$. When tracing the first qubit, the cross terms vanish because their first-qubit overlaps are zero. Therefore

$$\rho_2=\begin{pmatrix}|\alpha|^2&0\\0&|\beta|^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\rho_{\rm input}=\begin{pmatrix}|\alpha|^2&\alpha\beta^*\\\alpha^*\beta&|\beta|^2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Equality requires $\alpha\beta^*=0$. Since these are complex numbers, at least one amplitude must vanish. Normalisation then makes the input $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ up to a common phase. A general superposition is not copied into the second register.

2. Can one apparatus copy both $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$? Give a circuit.

Worked answer

Convert the known orthogonal alphabet into the computational basis, copy that basis, then convert both outputs back. With the second register initially zero, the two paths are

$$\begin{aligned}|+0\rangle&\xrightarrow{H\otimes I}|00\rangle\xrightarrow{\mathrm{CNOT}}|00\rangle\xrightarrow{H\otimes H}|++\rangle,\\|-0\rangle&\xrightarrow{H\otimes I}|10\rangle\xrightarrow{\mathrm{CNOT}}|11\rangle\xrightarrow{H\otimes H}|--\rangle.\end{aligned}$$

The same circuit works for both promised inputs because they are orthogonal. Its success on this two-state alphabet does not extend to arbitrary superpositions of its members; linearity would again produce correlated outputs instead of two independent copies.
