Superposition & the qubit

The superposition principle is the first axiom a physics course states and the last one it explains. Read once more, with a two-dimensional space in mind, it is the qubit.

The resultN&C §1.2, §2.1–2.2 — the qubit, the Bloch sphere, the tensor product

Move 1 In the course

Landau & Lifshitz open the whole subject not with a wave equation but with a principle. If a system can be found in a state described by $\psi_1$, and also in a state described by $\psi_2$, then it can be found in any state

$$ \psi \;=\; c_1\,\psi_1 + c_2\,\psi_2, \qquad c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{C}. $$

This is the principle of superposition (L&L §2), and everything algebraic in the theory follows from taking it literally: the states of a system are the vectors of a complex linear space, and physical quantities are Hermitian operators on that space (L&L §3). Two features of this space matter for us. First, the overall scale and phase of $\psi$ carry no physical meaning — $\psi$ and $\lambda\psi$ for any nonzero $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ describe the same state — because measurable predictions come from ratios of the coefficients, the $|c_i|^2$ of L&L §2. Second, an observable with two possible values supplies a pair of basis states, and superposition then fills in everything between them.

The cleanest physical instance is the one L&L reach in the chapter on spin: an electron's spin projection along an axis takes exactly two values, $+\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $-\tfrac{1}{2}$ (in units of $\hbar$). Call the corresponding states $\psi_\uparrow$ and $\psi_\downarrow$. Superposition says the general spin state is $c_1\psi_\uparrow + c_2\psi_\downarrow$, and the space of such states is exactly two complex dimensions. Nothing in this paragraph mentions computation. And yet it is already the entire stage.

Move 2 The step across

The step is to stop asking what the two states are and start asking what they can carry. A classical bit is a system with two distinguishable configurations, labelled $0$ and $1$. A spin-$\tfrac12$ has two distinguishable configurations too — but the superposition principle insists that, unlike a bit, it also occupies every complex combination of them. Rename the basis to match the intent:

$$ \psi_\uparrow \;\rightsquigarrow\; |0\rangle, \qquad \psi_\downarrow \;\rightsquigarrow\; |1\rangle. $$

The labels are now bit values, but the object is still a spin. This single relabelling is the crossing: a two-level quantum system, viewed as a carrier of one bit's worth of distinguishable alternatives together with all their superpositions, is a qubit. Everything Nielsen & Chuang say about it is forced by the two features from Move 1 — normalization-up-to-scale and the two-dimensionality — applied to this relabelled basis.

Take the general state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ with $\alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{C}$. Because scale is unphysical we may fix the norm,

$$ |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1, $$

and because an overall phase is unphysical we may spend one of the remaining real parameters. Write $\alpha = e^{i\gamma}\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}$ and $\beta = e^{i(\gamma+\varphi)}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}$ — always possible for normalized coefficients — and discard the common phase $e^{i\gamma}$:

$$ |\psi\rangle \;=\; \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|0\rangle \;+\; e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\,|1\rangle, \qquad \theta \in [0,\pi],\ \varphi \in [0,2\pi). $$

Two real angles remain. That is the whole content of the step across: the abstract two-dimensional superposition space of L&L, once its unphysical scale and phase are removed, is a sphere.

Move 3 The result

Nielsen & Chuang give this sphere a name (§1.2). The angles $(\theta,\varphi)$ are the polar and azimuthal coordinates of a point on the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the Bloch sphere, and the qubit state corresponds to the unit vector

$$ \hat{n} = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta). $$

The dictionary is exact and physical: the Cartesian components of $\hat n$ are the expectation values of the three Pauli operators in the state $|\psi\rangle$,

$$ n_x = \langle\psi|\sigma_x|\psi\rangle,\quad n_y = \langle\psi|\sigma_y|\psi\rangle,\quad n_z = \langle\psi|\sigma_z|\psi\rangle, $$

so a qubit's location on the sphere is literally the average spin direction — the same Pauli matrices L&L write down for spin-$\tfrac12$. The poles are the basis states: $\theta=0$ gives $|0\rangle$ (north), $\theta=\pi$ gives $|1\rangle$ (south). Antipodal points are orthogonal states; every other point is a genuine superposition. A classical bit could sit only at the two poles. The qubit has the entire surface.

A worked point

Set $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$, $\varphi = 0$. Then $\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} = \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and

$$ |{+}\rangle \;=\; \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle + |1\rangle\big), $$

with $\hat n = (1,0,0)$: it points along $+x$, on the equator, exactly between the poles. It is an equal superposition, and a $\sigma_z$ measurement gives $0$ or $1$ with probability $|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|^2 = \tfrac12$ each — the $|c_i|^2$ rule of Move 1, now read as a coin flip encoded in a spin.

Two qubits, and why the space explodes

The last piece N&C fix in §2.2 is how systems combine. In the course, the state of two independent subsystems is built from the products $\psi_i\chi_j$ of their basis states; the joint space is their tensor product. For two qubits this is $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{C}^4$, with basis

$$ |0\rangle\!\otimes\!|0\rangle,\quad |0\rangle\!\otimes\!|1\rangle,\quad |1\rangle\!\otimes\!|0\rangle,\quad |1\rangle\!\otimes\!|1\rangle \;\equiv\; |00\rangle, |01\rangle, |10\rangle, |11\rangle, $$

and superposition now runs over all four amplitudes at once. Crucially the dimension multiplies: $n$ qubits live in $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$, a space needing $2^n$ complex amplitudes to describe. This exponential is not new physics — it is the tensor product of L&L §5 and the composite-system rule, counted for $n$ two-level systems. It is also the reason a quantum computer is worth building, and the thread the rest of Part I follows: the states that are not products $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ are the entangled ones, and they are the subject of Essay 07.

Move 4 Reading

Course side. Landau & Lifshitz, Quantum Mechanics (Vol. 3): §2 for the superposition principle, §3–4 for operators, §5 for combining systems, and the spin chapter (Ch VIII) for the two-level prototype and the Pauli matrices.

Result side. Nielsen & Chuang, §1.2 for the qubit and the Bloch sphere, §2.1–2.2 for the state-space and tensor-product postulates. The Bloch-sphere parametrization appears at the start of §1.2; the tensor-product multiplication of dimensions is stated in §2.2.8.

Check the crossing. Confirm for yourself that $\langle\psi|\sigma_z|\psi\rangle = \cos\theta$ for $|\psi\rangle = \cos\tfrac\theta2|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2|1\rangle$, using $\sigma_z = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$. That one line is the entire bridge between "average spin along $z$" and "height on the Bloch sphere."
