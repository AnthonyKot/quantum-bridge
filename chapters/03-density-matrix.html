The density matrix, and the state of a part

This is the essay where the course was already right. Landau & Lifshitz write the density matrix as the state of a subsystem — the modern object, decades early. What quantum information changes is not the definition but the occasion for reaching for it.

→
The resultN&C §2.4 — mixed states, the reduced density operator, the partial trace

Move 1 In the course

Four essays in, it is worth admitting that the course does not always fall short — sometimes it hands you the finished object and only the use has to change. The density matrix is that case. Landau & Lifshitz introduce it in §14, and they introduce it, strikingly, as the correct description of a system that is part of a larger one with which it has interacted. When a subsystem cannot be assigned a wavefunction of its own, its state is a Hermitian operator

$$ \rho \;=\; \sum_i p_i\,|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|, \qquad \rho = \rho^\dagger,\quad \rho \ge 0,\quad \operatorname{Tr}\rho = 1, $$

and every prediction comes from it by the same rule that governed wavefunctions: the expectation of an observable $\hat A$ is $\langle \hat A\rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(\rho\,\hat A)$. The density matrix cleanly separates two situations that a wavefunction cannot tell apart. If $\rho = |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ then $\rho^2 = \rho$ and $\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2 = 1$: the state is pure, a single ray. Otherwise $\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2 < 1$ and the state is mixed. That number, the purity, is the whole diagnostic.

Notice how close this already is to the modern view. L&L do not present $\rho$ only as bookkeeping for a badly-known state; they present it as what the state of a part genuinely is. The object needs no repair. The question the course leaves open is only: when is a system a part, rather than a whole?

Move 2 The step across

The crossing here is not a fix but a shift in default. In the course, one reaches for $\rho$ in special circumstances — a beam of atoms from a hot source, a state prepared with classical uncertainty, an ensemble one is ignorant about. The mixedness reads as ignorance: the system really is in some definite $|\psi_i\rangle$, and $\rho$ averages over which. Quantum information takes the opposite circumstance as normal. A qubit that has ever interacted with anything — and no real qubit has not — is entangled with a partner you do not hold, and then it has no pure state of its own, not through ignorance but as a matter of principle. Its density matrix is the complete and correct description, and there is nothing underneath it to be ignorant of.

The construction that produces it is one we already built. In Essay 02 we coupled a system to an ancilla, measured the enlarged system, and read off the marginal statistics on the system alone; what survived was a POVM. Run the very same picture with the measurement removed. A pure joint state $|\Psi\rangle_{AB}$, described by $\rho_{AB} = |\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|$, is handed down to subsystem $A$ by summing the partner $B$ out of the description — the partial trace:

$$ \rho_A \;=\; \operatorname{Tr}_B\big(|\Psi\rangle\langle\Psi|\big) \;=\; \sum_j \big(I_A \otimes \langle j|_B\big)\,\rho_{AB}\,\big(I_A \otimes |j\rangle_B\big). $$

The symmetry with Essay 02 is exact, and it is the point of this essay:

A POVM is what a projective measurement looks like after tracing out the ancilla. A mixed state is what a pure state looks like after tracing out the ancilla. One operation — the partial trace over an inaccessible partner — casts two shadows.

Move 3 The result

Nielsen & Chuang make $\rho$ a first-class citizen (§2.4): the state postulate itself is restated for density operators, with mixedness measured by $\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2 \le 1$, and the reduced density operator and partial trace defined for subsystems (§2.4.3). The definitions are L&L's; the emphasis is inverted. Watch what the inversion buys on the smallest possible example.

A worked example: the part of a Bell state is blank

Take the maximally entangled pure state of two qubits, $|\beta_{00}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle + |11\rangle)$, and ask what qubit $A$ alone looks like. Expand the projector and trace out $B$:

$$ \rho_A = \operatorname{Tr}_B\!\Big[\tfrac12\big(|00\rangle+|11\rangle\big)\big(\langle00|+\langle11|\big)\Big] = \tfrac12\big(|0\rangle\langle0| + |1\rangle\langle1|\big) = \tfrac{I}{2}. $$

The cross terms $|00\rangle\langle11|$ and $|11\rangle\langle00|$ vanish under the trace because $\langle 1|0\rangle = 0$, and what remains is the maximally mixed state, $\rho_A = I/2$, with purity $\operatorname{Tr}\rho_A^2 = \tfrac12$. Read what that says. The joint state is pure and perfectly definite — measure $A$ and $B$ in the same basis and their outcomes always agree. Yet each half, examined alone, is a fair coin: maximally uncertain, carrying no state of its own. The whole is sharp; the parts are blank. Compare a mere product $|0\rangle_A|0\rangle_B$, whose reduced state is the pure $|0\rangle\langle0|$: there the part keeps a state. So the mixedness of $\rho_A$ is not ignorance about $A$ — it is the exact signature of $A$'s entanglement with $B$, and its purity is a first measure of how entangled they are.

The change of use, stated plainly

Nothing in this example contradicts L&L. The course computes $\rho$ for a subsystem; so do we. What moved is the sense of the normal case. For the course, a qubit is pure until proven statistical, and $\rho$ is the tool you fetch when knowledge runs out. For quantum information, a qubit is entangled until proven isolated, and $\rho$ is simply its state — because the partner is out there, held by the environment or an eavesdropper or the far end of a Bell pair, and it is not coming back. The density matrix did not need new content. It needed to be promoted from the exception to the rule.

Move 4 Reading

Course side — credit where due. Landau & Lifshitz §14, the density matrix, introduced as the state of a subsystem of a larger system — genuinely close to the modern reduced-state view. This essay names no gap in the course, because there is none to name; the honest report is that L&L had the object and quantum information changed the occasion for using it.

Result side. Nielsen & Chuang §2.4 for the density operator (with §2.4.1 on ensembles and §2.4.2 on its general properties — $\rho \ge 0$, $\operatorname{Tr}\rho = 1$, $\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2 \le 1$), and §2.4.3 for the reduced density operator and the partial trace used above.

How it connects. This is the twin of Essay 02: tracing out a partner turns a pure state into a mixed one exactly as it turns a projective measurement into a POVM. The Schmidt decomposition (Essay 07) will make the pairing precise, showing $\rho_A$ and $\rho_B$ share the same spectrum; and the von Neumann entropy $S(\rho_A) = -\operatorname{Tr}\rho_A\log\rho_A$ (Essay 15) will turn the purity into a real number of bits of entanglement — $1$ bit for the Bell state above.

Check the crossing. Reproduce $\rho_A = I/2$ for $|\beta_{00}\rangle$ by tracing out $B$ term by term, then confirm $\operatorname{Tr}\rho_A^2 = \tfrac12 < 1$. For contrast, trace out $B$ from a product state and watch the purity return to $1$. The distance of $\operatorname{Tr}\rho_A^2$ below $1$ is the distance of the joint state from being a product.
