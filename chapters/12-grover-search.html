Chapter 12

Grover search as two reflections

Grover search increases the probability of a marked answer by repeatedly reflecting amplitudes. A two-dimensional calculation gives both the speedup and the stopping rule.

Specify the search problem

Let $N=2^n\ge2$ candidate strings contain exactly one marked item $w$. The oracle recognises it coherently: $O_w|x\rangle=(-1)^{[x=w]}|x\rangle$. Here $[x=w]$ is one if the equality holds and zero otherwise. It can be obtained from a reversible Boolean checker using the $|-\rangle$ target of Chapter 10.

Prepare $|s\rangle=N^{-1/2}\sum_x|x\rangle$ with $H^{\otimes n}$. A direct measurement finds $w$ with probability $1/N$. One oracle call flips its amplitude's sign but leaves every probability unchanged. The next operation must convert that phase difference into a probability difference.

The diffusion operator reflects amplitudes about their mean

Define $D=2|s\rangle\langle s|-I$. It fixes the component along $|s\rangle$ and negates every orthogonal component, so $D^2=I$. If $|\psi\rangle=\sum_xa_x|x\rangle$ and $\bar a=N^{-1}\sum_xa_x$, then

$$D|\psi\rangle=\sum_x(2\bar a-a_x)|x\rangle.$$

This formula works for complex amplitudes too; $\bar a$ is an amplitude mean, not a probability mean. The circuit identity $D=H^{\otimes n}(2|0^n\rangle\langle0^n|-I)H^{\otimes n}$ shows that diffusion does not require knowledge of $w$. The marked-item information enters only through the oracle.

One iteration with four candidates

Take $N=4,w=2$. The initial amplitudes are $(1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2)$. The oracle gives $(1/2,1/2,-1/2,1/2)$, whose mean is $1/4$. Diffusion maps each amplitude to $1/2-a_x$: the three unmarked amplitudes become zero and the marked one becomes one. A single iteration $G=DO_w$ therefore finds the answer with certainty.

The example makes the sequence explicit: a sign change first, then cancellation on unmarked states and reinforcement on the marked state. Measuring between these two operations would prevent that interference.

Reduce the general problem to a plane

Let $|r\rangle=(N-1)^{-1/2}\sum_{x\ne w}|x\rangle$, orthogonal to $|w\rangle$. The initial state is

$$|s\rangle=\sin\theta|w\rangle+\cos\theta|r\rangle,\qquad \sin\theta=\frac1{\sqrt N}.$$

We can calculate diffusion in this basis directly from its definition. The projector is the column for $|s\rangle$ multiplied by its row:

$$|s\rangle\langle s|=\begin{pmatrix}\sin^2\theta&\sin\theta\cos\theta\\\sin\theta\cos\theta&\cos^2\theta\end{pmatrix}.$$

Multiplying by two and subtracting the identity gives diagonal entries $2\sin^2\theta-1=-\cos2\theta$ and $2\cos^2\theta-1=\cos2\theta$. Its off-diagonal entries are $2\sin\theta\cos\theta=\sin2\theta$. In particular, diffusion mixes marked and unmarked amplitudes; it is not diagonal in this basis.

The oracle is diagonal here because it negates $|w\rangle$ and leaves every unmarked basis state, hence $|r\rangle$, unchanged. To form $G=DO_w$, multiply $D$ by this diagonal matrix on the right: negate the first column of $D$ and retain the second. This explains each sign in the rotation matrix below.

Both oracle and diffusion preserve this plane. In the ordered basis $(|w\rangle,|r\rangle)$, write $|s\rangle$ as the real column $(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)^T$. The two individual matrices are

$$O_w=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad D=\begin{pmatrix}-\cos2\theta&\sin2\theta\\\sin2\theta&\cos2\theta\end{pmatrix}.$$

Multiplying the two reflections in circuit order gives

$$G=\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta&\sin2\theta\\-\sin2\theta&\cos2\theta\end{pmatrix}.$$

Multiplying $G$ by $(\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)^T$ gives $(\sin(\alpha+2\theta),\cos(\alpha+2\theta))^T$. Thus after $k$ iterations,

$$G^k|s\rangle=\sin((2k+1)\theta)|w\rangle+\cos((2k+1)\theta)|r\rangle,\quad p_k=\sin^2((2k+1)\theta).$$

Writing the plane in terms of $|w\rangle$ is for analysis; the circuit never needs to prepare that unknown basis vector directly.

Choose when to stop

The first probability maximum occurs near $(2k+1)\theta=\pi/2$. Choose a nonnegative integer nearest to $\pi/(4\theta)-1/2$. For large $N$, $\theta\approx1/\sqrt N$, giving $k\approx(\pi/4)\sqrt N$. Rounding introduces at most an angular error $\theta$, so for this single-target setting the resulting success probability is at least $\cos^2\theta=1-1/N$.

For $N=8$, the initial success chance is $1/8$. One iteration raises it to $25/32\approx0.781$; two raise it to $121/128\approx0.945$; three reduce it to $169/512\approx0.330$. Continuing indefinitely is not harmless. The state rotates past the target and its overlap falls again.

If $1\le M<N$ targets are marked, replace $|w\rangle$ by the uniform superposition of targets and use $\sin\theta=\sqrt{M/N}$. The same rotation formula follows, but the stopping count depends on $M$. An unknown target count requires an additional strategy; the single-target prescription cannot simply be assumed.

What the square-root speedup counts

We use asymptotic notation to compare resource growth as $N$ increases. A query count $Q(N)=O(g(N))$ has an upper bound $C g(N)$ for sufficiently large $N$, for a constant $C$ independent of $N$. Writing $Q(N)=\Theta(g(N))$ means there are also a positive lower constant $c$ and bounds $c g(N)\le Q(N)\le C g(N)$. Thus a $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ claim combines an algorithm using at most a constant times $\sqrt N$ queries with a theorem excluding asymptotically smaller counts under the same problem assumptions.

For unstructured search with a unique target and bounded error, the oracle-query complexity is $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ quantumly versus $\Theta(N)$ classically. The lower bound is a theorem, not proved by the two-reflection calculation. This is a quadratic improvement, not an exponential one.

Total runtime includes the reversible checker, diffusion, preparation, and final verification of the candidate. If data must first be loaded into a memory, account for that cost and whether it is reused across searches. If the candidates are defined implicitly and the predicate is inexpensive, the oracle model can better reflect the task. Neither the existence of a checker nor the query bound alone establishes a practical advantage.

Problems

1. Reproduce $p_1=25/32$ for $N=8$ using mean reflection.

Worked answer

Write each initial amplitude as $a=1/\sqrt8$. After the oracle there are seven $+a$ amplitudes and one $-a$, so the sum is $6a$ and the mean is $6a/8=3a/4$.

$$a'_w=2(3a/4)-(-a)=5a/2,\qquad a'_{x\ne w}=2(3a/4)-a=a/2.$$

Squaring the marked amplitude gives $p_1=25a^2/4=25/32$. The seven others contribute $7a^2/4=7/32$, checking total probability one. The probability gain comes from the amplitude update; squaring before averaging would give the wrong result.

2. For $N=4$ with one target, what happens after a second iteration?

Worked answer

For four candidates, $\sin\theta=1/2$, so $\theta=\pi/6$. After the first iteration the marked amplitude is $\sin(3\theta)=1$, and all others are zero. After the second,

$$p_2=\sin^2((2\cdot2+1)\theta)=\sin^2(5\pi/6)=1/4.$$

One can also check directly: starting from $|w\rangle$, the next oracle gives marked amplitude $-1$ and unmarked amplitudes zero. Their mean is $-1/4$. Diffusion makes the marked amplitude $1/2$ and each unmarked amplitude $-1/2$, so all four probabilities are $1/4$. The algorithm has already passed its first optimum.
