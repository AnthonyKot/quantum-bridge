Bloch waves, and the eigenvalue that becomes an answer

The Bloch wave a course writes down to label a state in a periodic lattice is, letter for letter, the quantum Fourier transform of a position state. Period-finding runs the very same diagonalization — and asks the eigenvalue to be the answer instead of a label.

In the courseL&L §15 (momentum generates translations) + Bloch's theorem — Bloch waves diagonalize translations on $\mathbb{Z}_N$
→
The resultN&C §5.1, §5.2, §5.4.1 — the QFT, phase estimation, period-finding

Move 1 In the course

Momentum, in the course, is the generator of spatial translation (L&L §15): the operator that shifts a state by $a$ is $\hat T_a = e^{-ia\hat p/\hbar}$, and its eigenstates are the plane waves $e^{ikx}$. To diagonalize translations is to pass to the momentum — the Fourier — basis. Now make the translations discrete. In a periodic lattice of $N$ sites with periodic boundary conditions, the translations form the finite cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}_N$, and Bloch's theorem gives the simultaneous eigenstates of every lattice shift:

$$ |k\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{x=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i k x / N}\,|x\rangle, \qquad k = 0,1,\dots,N-1, $$

labelled by the crystal momentum $k$; the shift $\hat S:|x\rangle\mapsto|x+1\rangle$ acts on $|k\rangle$ by the pure phase $e^{-2\pi i k/N}$. The course computes these Bloch waves to name the stationary states of an electron in a crystal. And here is the whole essay in one observation: that sum is identically the discrete Fourier transform of the position state $|x\rangle$. The reader has already written the quantum Fourier transform. It was called a Bloch wave.

Where this sits. Momentum as the generator of translations is Volume 3 (§15), verified. Bloch's theorem for a periodic lattice is standard course material but lives in the solid-state / statistical side of the curriculum, outside the Volume 3 this book checks against — so it is cited by topic only, unverified, on the same footing as the thermodynamics of Essay 15.

Move 2 The step across

The mathematics is settled; what moves is the purpose of the eigenvalue. The physicist diagonalizes translations in order to label: crystal momentum $k$ is a quantum number, a tag hung on a stationary state, and the eigenvalue is bookkeeping. Period-finding diagonalizes the same translations because the eigenvalue is the output. Given a function $f$ with an unknown period $r$, its periodicity makes the relevant state invariant under translation by $r$; so in the Bloch basis the amplitude concentrates on the momenta $k$ that a period $r$ forces — near multiples of $N/r$ — and a single measurement in that basis returns the period, read out of the phase. Same change of basis, exactly; the eigenvalue has simply moved from label to answer.

It is the same inversion Essay 05 made when a spin's precession became a gate you apply: the machinery is untouched and only the intent turns over. The general form of the trick is phase estimation — extract the eigenphase of a unitary — and period-finding is phase estimation aimed at a shift.

Move 3 The result

Nielsen & Chuang make the Bloch transform a gate. The quantum Fourier transform (§5.1) is the unitary

$$ |x\rangle \;\longmapsto\; \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i x k/N}\,|k\rangle, $$

the change of basis of Move 1, and its circuit uses only $O(n^2)$ elementary gates on $n=\log_2 N$ qubits. Phase estimation (§5.2) builds on it: to learn the phase $\varphi$ of an eigenstate $U|u\rangle = e^{2\pi i\varphi}|u\rangle$, apply the controlled powers $U^{2^j}$ into a register and run the inverse QFT, which spells $\varphi$ out in binary. Period-finding (§5.4.1) is that procedure pointed at the shift, and it returns $r$. (Those controlled powers $U^{2^j}$ are exactly the arbitrary unitaries Essay 04 guarantees can be built from a finite gate set — universality is what makes the phase-estimation circuit legal.)

The QFT is not a fast FFT

This is the point almost every short account blurs, and stating it plainly is worth a paragraph. The QFT's $O(n^2)$ gates do stand against the fast Fourier transform's $O(N\log N)$ operations — exponentially fewer, since $N = 2^n$. But this is not "we compute Fourier transforms faster," because you cannot read the transformed amplitudes. The QFT leaves the Fourier coefficients as the amplitudes of a quantum state, and a measurement returns a single sample $k$ drawn from the transformed distribution $|\tilde f(k)|^2$ — one draw, not the array. There is no way to extract the whole spectrum; the classical FFT hands you every coefficient, and the QFT never will. What the QFT actually does is rotate into a basis where one measurement is likely to hand you the one number you were after. That is useless for signal processing and decisive for period-finding, where the answer is a single integer hiding in the spectrum. The speedup is real; it is the speedup of a perfectly aimed sample, not of a faster transform.

Where this essay stops

Period-finding is the end of the crossing. Turning it into Shor's factoring algorithm — reducing factoring to order-finding, and pulling the exact order out of an approximate phase with continued fractions — is number theory, and it has no course side at all. It is not a bridge back to the physics course; it is an import from another discipline, and this book's method is to trace results back to the course, not to narrate mathematics the course never touches. So the completion is pointed to — Nielsen & Chuang §5.3, order-finding and factoring — and left there. Naming the boundary is the same discipline as naming where the physics canon falls short, run the other way: here the canon reaches all the way to the transform, and the number theory is simply not its territory to claim.

Move 4 Reading

Course side. Landau & Lifshitz §15 for momentum as the generator of translations, with plane waves as the eigenstates — verified, Volume 3. For the discrete case, Bloch's theorem in a periodic lattice, where the eigenstates are the Bloch waves that are the discrete Fourier transform; that is standard solid-state material outside Volume 3, so it is cited by topic only and flagged unverified.

Result side. Nielsen & Chuang §5.1 for the quantum Fourier transform, §5.2 for phase estimation, and §5.4.1 for period-finding. The number-theoretic completion of Shor's algorithm is §5.3, deliberately outside this essay's scope.

Scope, three fences. No success-probability analysis, no continued fractions, and no reduction of factoring to order-finding. The essay ends at the transform and the period.

How it connects. The eigenvalue-becomes-output inversion is the same shape as the precession-becomes-gate of Essay 05, and the controlled powers phase estimation needs are what Essay 04's universality lets you build.

Check the crossing. Confirm that $|k\rangle = N^{-1/2}\sum_x e^{2\pi i kx/N}|x\rangle$ is at once the Bloch eigenstate of the shift $\hat S$ (with eigenvalue $e^{-2\pi i k/N}$) and the quantum Fourier transform of $|x\rangle$. Then confirm the cost claim honestly: $O(n^2)$ gates, but a single sample from $|\tilde f(k)|^2$ on measurement — not the spectrum.
