Chapter 02

Measurement: probabilities and state changes

A qubit prepared in $(\sqrt3|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)/2$ is measured and reads 0, as it will three times in four. What state is it in now, and do the outcome probabilities decide that?

One preparation, two branches

Take the state from Chapter 1, $|\psi\rangle=(\sqrt3|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)/2$, and measure it in the computational basis. The Born rule gives two branches: the reading 0 with probability $3/4$, and the reading 1 with probability $1/4$. That answers one question. A second question is what the qubit is afterwards, if it still exists: a photon counter, for example, absorbs the photon it counts.

For an ideal measurement, the qubit is left in $|0\rangle$ after the reading 0 and in $|1\rangle$ after the reading 1. The evidence is operational. Repeat the same measurement immediately and it gives the same reading every time. The state after a reading is called the conditional state: conditional on that outcome having occurred and been recorded.

The probabilities come from the preparation and the question asked. The conditional state also depends on what the apparatus does to the system, so it is a separate piece of information. This chapter makes that separation precise, first for ideal measurements and then for general ones.

Projectors and conditional states

A projector is a matrix $P$ with $P^\dagger=P$ and $P^2=P$. It maps every vector to its component in a fixed subspace and leaves that component unchanged; $|0\rangle\langle0|$ applied to $|\psi\rangle$ keeps $(\sqrt3/2)|0\rangle$. An ideal projective measurement is described by mutually orthogonal projectors $P_m$, one for each outcome, with $P_mP_n=\delta_{mn}P_m$ ($\delta_{mn}$ is one when $m=n$ and zero otherwise) and $\sum_mP_m=I$. A projector may have rank greater than one, meaning that it projects onto a subspace of dimension greater than one. For a pure input state,

$$p(m)=\langle\psi|P_m|\psi\rangle,\qquad |\psi_m\rangle=\frac{P_m|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{p(m)}}.$$

In the example, $P_0|\psi\rangle=(\sqrt3/2)|0\rangle$ has squared norm $3/4$, which is $p(0)$. Dividing by $\sqrt{3/4}$ gives the conditional state $|0\rangle$. The second expression is defined only for an outcome with nonzero probability; its denominator restores norm one. This is the ideal (Lüders) update. The same outcome statistics can be accompanied by additional physical disturbance, as the apparatus called N below shows.

Repeating the ideal measurement after outcome 0 gives 0 with certainty because $P_0^2=P_0$. A degenerate measurement, one with a projector of rank greater than one, behaves differently from measuring a complete basis. On two qubits, even parity uses $P_{\rm even}=|00\rangle\langle00|+|11\rangle\langle11|$. It preserves $\alpha|00\rangle+\beta|11\rangle$ unchanged; separate measurements of the two bits would destroy the coherence between those terms.

Recorded and ignored outcomes

A conditional state is the description for someone who knows the outcome. Suppose instead that the measurement is made and its reading discarded unread. Later predictions must then average over the branches: each branch's probability times the prediction made from its conditional state. Probabilities are averaged, not amplitudes, because the apparatus now holds a record that distinguishes the branches, whether or not anyone reads it.

Take $|+\rangle$, source A of Chapter 1. Measured in the $+/-$ basis it gives $+$ every time. Now let it first pass through a computational-basis measurement whose reading is thrown away. The branch $|0\rangle$ gives $+$ with probability $1/2$, and so does the branch $|1\rangle$, so the average is $1/2$. For every later measurement on the qubit alone, the unread measurement has made source A equivalent to source C: the relative phase that produced the certain outcome is gone from the qubit. The qubit and apparatus together are a different matter. The apparatus still holds a record correlated with the qubit, and a measurement that included the apparatus could detect that correlation. No single state vector describes the qubit on its own. Chapter 3 constructs the object that does; this chapter needs only the averaging rule.

Introducing a measuring apparatus

To see where conditional states come from, model the apparatus as a second quantum system. Start with a concrete case. A pointer qubit $A$ starts in $|0\rangle_A$ and interacts with the system through a unitary, a reversible evolution that preserves inner products (Chapter 4 treats these). This one flips the pointer when the system is 1:

$$|0\rangle|0\rangle_A\mapsto|0\rangle|0\rangle_A,\qquad |1\rangle|0\rangle_A\mapsto|1\rangle|1\rangle_A.$$

By linearity, the running example becomes $(\sqrt3/2)|0\rangle|0\rangle_A+(i/2)|1\rangle|1\rangle_A$. Reading the pointer gives 0 with probability $3/4$ and leaves the system in $|0\rangle$: the ideal measurement, obtained from an interaction and a reading of the pointer. Chapter 4 calls this interaction controlled-NOT.

In general, prepare an auxiliary system in $|0\rangle_A$, couple it to the system by a unitary $U$, and read its orthonormal pointer basis $\{|m\rangle_A\}$. Define an operator on the original system by taking the apparatus matrix element,

$$M_m=(I\otimes\langle m|_A)U(I\otimes|0\rangle_A).$$

There are three spaces to distinguish in this expression. $I\otimes|0\rangle_A$ attaches the prepared apparatus to any system vector. $U$ acts on the resulting joint vector. Finally, $I\otimes\langle m|_A$ extracts the component with pointer value $m$, leaving a vector in the system space. Their composition is therefore a linear map from the system space back to itself: the operator $M_m$.

For example, if the joint output has the form $|v_0\rangle_S|0\rangle_A+|v_1\rangle_S|1\rangle_A$, applying $I\otimes\langle0|_A$ leaves $|v_0\rangle_S$. The other term vanishes because $\langle0|1\rangle=0$. The vectors $v_0,v_1$ need not be normalised: their squared norms are the two pointer probabilities. This is why the selected branch must later be divided by its norm. In the running example, $|v_0\rangle=(\sqrt3/2)|0\rangle$ and $|v_1\rangle=(i/2)|1\rangle$, so $M_0=|0\rangle\langle0|$ and $M_1=|1\rangle\langle1|$.

The joint output is $\sum_m M_m|\psi\rangle\otimes|m\rangle_A$. Its norm is one for every normalised input, so $\sum_mM_m^\dagger M_m=I$. Projecting the apparatus onto $m$ selects the vector $M_m|\psi\rangle$. Therefore

$$p(m)=\langle\psi|M_m^\dagger M_m|\psi\rangle,\qquad |\psi_m\rangle=\frac{M_m|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{p(m)}}.$$

The operators $E_m=M_m^\dagger M_m$ are called effects. Each is positive, written $E_m\ge0$, meaning $\langle v|E_m|v\rangle\ge0$ for every vector $v$. Their collection is a positive operator-valued measure, or POVM: $E_m\ge0$ and $\sum_mE_m=I$. These conditions guarantee nonnegative probabilities summing to one. Effects need not be orthogonal projectors, and there may be more effects than the system dimension.

Completeness from the apparatus basis, without probabilities

Insert the full apparatus basis between $U^\dagger$ and $U$:

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_mM_m^\dagger M_m &=(I\otimes\langle0|)U^\dagger\left(I\otimes\sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|\right)U(I\otimes|0\rangle)\\ &=(I\otimes\langle0|)U^\dagger U(I\otimes|0\rangle)=I. \end{aligned}$$

The first simplification uses an exhaustive list of pointer outcomes. The second uses reversible joint evolution and a normalised initial apparatus. The general measurement rule thus follows from ordinary evolution and projective readout on a larger system.

Why effects do not determine disturbance

Now follow the same interaction and reading by a bit flip $X$ on the system. Call this apparatus N. Its operators are $N_m=XM_m$: $N_0=|1\rangle\langle0|$ and $N_1=|0\rangle\langle1|$. The effects are unchanged, since $N_m^\dagger N_m=M_m^\dagger X^\dagger XM_m=M_m^\dagger M_m$. Every input therefore gives the same outcome probabilities under N as under M. For the running example N reads 0 with probability $3/4$, as M does, but leaves $|1\rangle$. An immediate second reading by N gives 1, not 0.

This answers the opening question. The probability of reading 0 was fixed by the preparation and the effect $E_0=|0\rangle\langle0|$. The state left behind was fixed by the apparatus, and two apparatuses with the same effects can leave different states. A POVM specifies outcome probabilities, not a unique state update.

In general $M_m=V_m\sqrt{E_m}$, where $\sqrt{E_m}$ is the positive matrix whose square is $E_m$ and $V_m$ is an outcome-dependent unitary; every such choice gives the same effects and potentially different conditional states. If one recorded outcome groups several unobserved apparatus outcomes, it requires several operators rather than one; Chapter 13 gives that update.

A measurement allowed to answer “inconclusive”

General effects allow measurements that no projective measurement on the qubit can perform. Suppose the input is either $|0\rangle$ or $|+\rangle$, with equal prior probabilities. These are not orthogonal, so no measurement identifies them every time without error. We want some outcomes that identify the state without error, while allowing other runs to report failure. Choose

$$E_+=c|1\rangle\langle1|,\quad E_0=c|-\rangle\langle-|,\quad E_?=I-E_+-E_0.$$

The label on an effect is the conclusion. $E_+$ cannot occur on input $|0\rangle$, and $E_0$ cannot occur on input $|+\rangle$. Each conclusive result has conditional probability $c/2$ on the input it identifies. To find an allowed $c$, write

$$|1\rangle\langle1|+|-\rangle\langle-|=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&-1/2\\-1/2&3/2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Its characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2-2\lambda+1/2$, so its eigenvalues are $1\pm1/\sqrt2$. Positivity of $E_?$ requires $1-c(1+1/\sqrt2)\ge0$. Taking the largest allowed value in this family gives $c=2-\sqrt2$, and hence $p(\text{success})=1-1/\sqrt2\approx0.293$. Failure is explicitly reported; it is not an incorrect identification. We have optimised this symmetric construction, without proving optimality over all measurements.

Every finite POVM has an apparatus construction

The apparatus model produced a POVM from an interaction and a pointer reading. The converse also holds: any finite collection of positive effects summing to $I$ can be realised by some interaction with an auxiliary system followed by a projective reading of that system. So the three-effect measurement above is physically allowed, not only mathematically consistent.

The construction

Given effects, define $V|\psi\rangle=\sum_m\sqrt{E_m}|\psi\rangle\otimes|m\rangle$. Then $V^\dagger V=\sum_mE_m=I$, so $V$ preserves inner products. In finite dimensions it can be extended to a unitary on a sufficiently large space by completing an orthonormal basis. Reading the apparatus realises the required POVM. This is a dilation construction. The original system is a tensor factor; identifying it with the initial subspace $\mathcal H_S\otimes|0\rangle_A$ is part of the construction, not a new physical postulate.

Where this leaves us

After reading 0, the running example is in $|0\rangle$ if the measurement is ideal, and in $|1\rangle$ under apparatus N, although both apparatuses read 0 with the same probability $3/4$. The outcome probabilities are fixed by the effects; the conditional state needs the measurement operators, which is more information. When a reading is made but not kept, predictions average over the branches. For measurements on the qubit alone, $|+\rangle$ then behaves like a preparation that no single state vector describes, while the apparatus keeps a correlated record.

That is source C again. It is also what a subsystem looks like when its partner is out of reach, as later chapters on entanglement need. The next chapter gives both situations one description.

Problems

1. Apply even/odd parity measurement to $|++\rangle$. Find both outcome probabilities and normalised conditional states.

Worked answer

Expand the input before applying either projector: $|++\rangle=(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle)/2$. Even parity keeps the equal-bit terms:

$$P_{\rm even}|++\rangle=(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/2,\qquad p_{\rm even}=1/4+1/4=1/2.$$

Divide this vector by $\sqrt{1/2}$ to obtain $(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Odd parity similarly gives unnormalised $(|01\rangle+|10\rangle)/2$, probability $1/2$, and normalised $(|01\rangle+|10\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Both branches retain a superposition because parity does not distinguish the two terms within the selected subspace.

The tempting wrong turn is to conclude that after the reading “even” the bits are individually $00$ or $11$. The measurement reports only that they agree. The conditional state keeps both terms with their amplitudes, and a later $X\otimes X$ measurement on it gives $+1$ with certainty, which neither $|00\rangle$ nor $|11\rangle$ alone would do.

2. Is $c=1$ allowed in the three-effect example?

Worked answer

Let $A=|1\rangle\langle1|+|-\rangle\langle-|$, whose eigenvalues are $1\pm1/\sqrt2$. The inconclusive effect is $E_?=I-cA$, so on an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ its eigenvalue is $1-c\lambda$.

$$\lambda_{\min}(E_?)=1-c(1+1/\sqrt2)\xrightarrow{c=1}-1/\sqrt2.$$

A normalised eigenvector with that eigenvalue would have outcome probability $\langle v|E_?|v\rangle=-1/\sqrt2$. Thus $c=1$ is invalid even though the three effects still sum to identity: positivity is the failing condition.
