Measurement, and the world outside the subspace

A course teaches projective measurement as the whole theory of observation. It is not the whole theory — it is the view from inside a subspace. Widen the space and a larger, simpler notion of measurement appears, and Naimark's theorem says nothing was ever left out.

Move 1 In the course

Landau & Lifshitz give measurement one section, §7, and it is decisive. To measure an observable is to name a Hermitian operator $\hat A$, expand the state in its orthonormal eigenbasis $\{|n\rangle\}$, and collapse: the outcome is an eigenvalue $a_n$ with probability $|\langle n|\psi\rangle|^2$, and the state is left in $|n\rangle$. Written with the projectors $P_n = |n\rangle\langle n|$, this is the projective (or von Neumann) measurement, and it carries three rigid features:

$$ P_n P_m = \delta_{nm} P_n, \qquad \sum_n P_n = I, \qquad p(n) = \langle\psi|P_n|\psi\rangle. $$

The projectors are orthogonal and idempotent. Two consequences follow that a course rarely flags as assumptions. First, orthogonality caps the number of distinct outcomes at the dimension $d$ of the space — a qubit measurement has at most two. Second, idempotence makes the measurement repeatable: measure again and you get the same $n$ with certainty. This is presented, in L&L and in every first course, as the account of measurement — most introductions stop exactly here, at the projective case, and never let on that a more general notion of measurement exists at all. The question is what that habit quietly assumes.

Move 2 The step across

It assumes the thing you measure is the whole world. It almost never is. Your qubit sits next to an ancilla, a probe, a stray mode of the apparatus; what you actually control and read out is a larger system, and the qubit is a subspace of it. So do the honest thing: adjoin an ancilla in a fixed state, let system and ancilla interact through a joint unitary $U$, and then perform an ordinary projective measurement — but only on the enlarged system. Ask what statistics the original qubit alone appears to obey.

Grinding through the algebra (a projective $\{P_m\}$ upstairs, pulled back through $U$ and the ancilla state down onto the qubit) leaves each outcome governed by an operator $E_m = \langle a|U^\dagger P_m U|a\rangle$ acting on the qubit, and these operators have lost the rigidity of Move 1. They need not be orthogonal. They need not be projectors, so the measurement need not be repeatable. There can be more of them than the dimension $d$. Exactly two properties survive the descent into the subspace:

$$ E_m \ge 0 \quad(\text{each is positive}), \qquad \sum_m E_m = I \quad(\text{they resolve the identity}), $$

with the outcome probabilities still $p(m) = \langle\psi|E_m|\psi\rangle$. That is the step across, and it is a loosening, not a new law: drop “orthogonal projector,” keep “positive operator summing to the identity.” The course was not wrong. It was computing inside a subspace and mistaking the walls of that subspace for the walls of the theory.

Move 3 The result

The surviving object is a POVM — a positive operator-valued measure — and it is the general measurement of Nielsen & Chuang, §2.2.6. A POVM is any set $\{E_m\}$ of positive operators with $\sum_m E_m = I$; the outcome probabilities are $p(m)=\langle\psi|E_m|\psi\rangle$. Projective measurement is the special case where the $E_m$ happen to be orthogonal projectors. The generalization earns its keep by doing something projective measurement provably cannot.

A worked example: telling apart states you shouldn't be able to

Alice hands Bob a qubit she promises is either $|0\rangle$ or $|{+}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$. These are not orthogonal — $\langle 0|{+}\rangle = 1/\sqrt2$ — and N&C's Box 2.3 proves no measurement can reliably tell them apart: none can always name which state it was handed. A projective measurement, forced to answer on every shot, must sometimes answer wrong; a POVM escapes by sometimes declining to answer at all. Take three elements, with $c = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{1+\sqrt2}$:

$$ E_1 = c\,|1\rangle\langle 1|, \qquad E_2 = c\,|{-}\rangle\langle{-}|, \qquad E_3 = I - E_1 - E_2, $$

where $|{-}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$. Check the logic. If the state was $|0\rangle$, then $\langle 0|E_1|0\rangle = c\,|\langle 0|1\rangle|^2 = 0$: outcome 1 is impossible for $|0\rangle$, so seeing outcome 1 means the state was certainly $|{+}\rangle$. Symmetrically, $\langle{+}|E_2|{+}\rangle \propto |\langle{+}|{-}\rangle|^2 = 0$, so outcome 2 certifies $|0\rangle$. Outcome 3 is the honest “don't know.” Bob is never wrong — he is only, sometimes, silent.

The constant is forced by positivity: $E_3 \ge 0$ requires $\det E_3 \ge 0$, and $c = \sqrt2/(1+\sqrt2) = 2-\sqrt2$ is exactly the value where $\det E_3 = 0$, the largest $c$ the constraint allows. At that value the inconclusive outcome has probability $\langle\psi|E_3|\psi\rangle = 1/\sqrt2$ for either input, so Bob gets a definite, never-mistaken answer with probability $1 - 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.29$ — precisely the optimal unambiguous-discrimination rate $1 - |\langle 0|{+}\rangle|$. No projective measurement on the qubit alone can match this; it takes the extra outcomes a POVM allows.

Naimark: nothing was actually added

Move 2 built a POVM by measuring projectively on a larger space. The remarkable converse — Naimark's dilation theorem — is that every POVM arises this way. Any general measurement on a system is a projective measurement on that system together with an ancilla, after a suitable unitary — a POVM is the shadow such a measurement casts back on the smaller space. N&C make this equivalence explicit in Box 2.5: projective measurement dressed with ancillas and unitaries, and the general POVM formalism, describe exactly the same physics. So the loosening of Move 2 added no new physics to the postulates — it revealed that the course's projective measurement was already the general one, seen through the keyhole of a subspace. The three extra outcomes Bob used were projective outcomes upstairs, in dimensions his course never mentioned.

Move 4 Reading

Course side — and the gap, named. Landau & Lifshitz §7, “The wave function and measurements,” for projective measurement — and note that it stops there, as does Sakurai and every standard course. This is not an oversight to tiptoe around; it is the essay's subject. N&C's own Box 2.5 gives the reason physicists skip it: in most real experiments the apparatus is too coarse to realize anything finer than a crude projective measurement, so the general formalism is rarely needed — which is exactly the admission that the course works in a subspace.

Result side. Nielsen & Chuang: §2.2.5 for projective measurements, §2.2.6 for POVMs, §2.2.4 with Box 2.3 for distinguishing states, and Box 2.5 (with the composite-system construction of §2.2.8) for the projective-measurement-on-a-larger-space equivalence — the content of Naimark's dilation theorem.

What this essay unblocks. POVMs are the vocabulary the rest of Part I and Part V are written in. The density operator (Essay 03) is the state on which POVMs act; quantum operations and Kraus operators (Essay 13) are barely more than a POVM with the post-measurement states kept; and accessible information and the Holevo bound (Essay 15) are statements about the best POVM. Fixing this notion here unblocks all three.

Check the crossing. Verify $\sum_m E_m = I$ for the three elements above, then confirm that demanding $E_3 \ge 0$ forces $c = \sqrt2/(1+\sqrt2)$ (compute $\det E_3$ in the $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ basis and set it to zero — it factors to $c^2 - 4c + 2 = 0$). The never-err discrimination is entirely in that one constraint.
