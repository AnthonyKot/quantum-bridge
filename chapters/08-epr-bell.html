Part III · Entanglement — Essay 08

EPR, and the theorem that made it physics

For thirty years the EPR argument was considered philosophy — a matter of taste about an untestable question. Then Bell turned it into a number. This is the one essay where the book's slogan bends, and it is more interesting for bending.

In the courseEPR (1935): quantum mechanics is incomplete, not wrong — a claim about local realism
→
The resultN&C §2.6 — the CHSH / Bell inequality and its quantum violation

A word before the four moves. The course canon barely reaches this essay, and it is honest to say so up front. Landau & Lifshitz contains no EPR argument and no Bell inequality; Feynman's two-state chapters do not either. Only Sakurai, of the standard texts, treats them head-on. So the "course side" here is not a textbook section but a 1935 paper — and that is the point. This is the crossing where old postulates, asked a new question, produce new physics.

Move 1 In the course

Start with what Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen actually argued, because it is not what the folklore says. EPR did not claim quantum mechanics is wrong, and they did not celebrate "spooky action at a distance." They argued the opposite: that quantum mechanics is incomplete, precisely because they wanted to keep locality. Their engine is a criterion of reality, which in paraphrase runs: if you can predict a quantity with certainty while leaving the system completely untouched, then something physically real — an element of reality — must already have fixed that value.

Take two spin-½ particles prepared in the singlet $|\beta_{11}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle - |10\rangle)$ (Bohm's sharpening of the original position–momentum version) and let them fly apart. Measure particle A's spin along $z$: the outcome fixes particle B's spin along $z$ with certainty, without touching B. By the criterion, B's $z$-spin is an element of reality. But you could equally have measured A along $x$, fixing B's $x$-spin — also, then, an element of reality. Locality says your free choice at A cannot physically alter B, so both B's $x$- and $z$-spin must have been real all along. Quantum mechanics, which forbids simultaneous sharp values of non-commuting $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z$, cannot describe both. Therefore — EPR conclude — the wavefunction is an incomplete description; there must be finer variables it omits.

Read the logic carefully: it is local realism $\Rightarrow$ hidden variables. Einstein's preference was to keep locality and add the missing variables, demoting quantum indeterminacy to ignorance of them. Nothing in the argument is testable. For three decades it read as a standoff between two consistent worldviews, settled by temperament.

Move 2 The step across

John Bell's move in 1964 is one of the great steps in physics, and it is exactly the kind this book is about: he took a postulate structure that was already on the table and asked an information-theoretic question of it — how strongly can the outcomes at two distant detectors be correlated? Crucially, he asked it of EPR's own hypothesis, not of quantum mechanics. Assume local hidden variables: each particle carries a shared instruction set $\lambda$, and each detector's outcome depends only on $\lambda$ and its own local setting, never on the distant setting. Then derive a constraint every such theory must satisfy.

The step across is the conversion of a philosophical posit — "elements of reality" — into a measurable ceiling on correlations. Once the posit predicts a number, and quantum mechanics predicts a different number, the standoff becomes an experiment. This is where reinterpreting the old postulates stops being a matter of clothing and starts producing physics no one knew was there.

Move 3 The result

The sharpest form is the CHSH inequality (Clauser, Horne, Shimony & Holt 1969), the version Nielsen & Chuang present in §2.6. Alice picks one of two settings $a, a'$; Bob picks one of two settings $b, b'$; each measurement returns $\pm 1$. In a local hidden-variable theory the outcomes are functions $A(a,\lambda),\,B(b,\lambda) \in \{+1,-1\}$ of a shared variable $\lambda$ drawn from some distribution $p(\lambda)$, and the correlation is

$$ E(a,b) = \int A(a,\lambda)\,B(b,\lambda)\,p(\lambda)\,d\lambda. $$

Form the combination

$$ S = E(a,b) - E(a,b') + E(a',b) + E(a',b'). $$

The bound comes from a single line of arithmetic inside the integral. Group the terms:

$$ A(a,\lambda)\big[B(b,\lambda) - B(b',\lambda)\big] + A(a',\lambda)\big[B(b,\lambda) + B(b',\lambda)\big]. $$

Because $B(b,\lambda)$ and $B(b',\lambda)$ are each $\pm 1$, one bracket is $0$ and the other is $\pm 2$; the whole expression is therefore $\pm 2$ for every $\lambda$. Averaging cannot enlarge it, so any local hidden-variable theory obeys

$$ |S| \le 2. $$

What quantum mechanics predicts

Now compute $S$ for the singlet using nothing but the course's own spin machinery. Measuring $\boldsymbol\sigma\!\cdot\!\hat a$ on A and $\boldsymbol\sigma\!\cdot\!\hat b$ on B, the quantum correlation is

$$ E(a,b) = \langle \beta_{11}|\,(\boldsymbol\sigma\!\cdot\!\hat a)\otimes(\boldsymbol\sigma\!\cdot\!\hat b)\,|\beta_{11}\rangle = -\,\hat a\cdot\hat b = -\cos\theta_{ab}. $$

Choose four coplanar directions at $\hat a = 0^\circ$, $\hat a' = 90^\circ$, $\hat b = 45^\circ$, $\hat b' = 135^\circ$. Then $\theta_{ab}=\theta_{a'b}=\theta_{a'b'}=45^\circ$ while $\theta_{ab'}=135^\circ$, so

$$ S = -\cos 45^\circ - \big(-\cos 135^\circ\big) + \big(-\cos 45^\circ\big) + \big(-\cos 45^\circ\big) = -\tfrac{4}{\sqrt2} = -2\sqrt2. $$

Thus $|S| = 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83$, cleanly above the local-realist ceiling of $2$. The gap is not a rounding error or an idealization; $2\sqrt2$ is Tsirelson's bound, the exact maximum quantum mechanics allows. And it is real: Aspect's 1982 experiments saw the violation, and the loophole-free tests of 2015 closed the last escape routes. Nature sides with $2\sqrt2$.

Why this essay bends the slogan

The landing page says quantum computing "did not add new physics." Here is the one place that is arguably false, and it is worth confronting rather than hiding. EPR was a reinterpretation — the kind of crossing this book makes everywhere. But Bell's theorem is not a reinterpretation. It is a new empirical prediction: that the correlations of an entangled pair cannot be reproduced by any local theory with hidden variables, a fact about the physical world that no one before 1964 knew was even decidable. So the honest thesis is finer than the slogan:

Quantum information added no new postulates. But Bell shows that asking an information-theoretic question of the old postulates — how correlated can outcomes be? — can produce genuinely new physics: a prediction that rules out a whole class of theories, confirmed in the laboratory.

That is a stronger and more interesting claim than "nothing new here," and this essay is where the book earns it. The postulates were old; the question was new; the result was physics.

Move 4 Reading

Course side — and the gap, named. Neither Landau & Lifshitz nor Feynman's two-state chapters treat EPR or Bell; do not go looking for a section that is not there. Among the standard texts only Sakurai & Napolitano, Modern Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed., Napolitano) treats them head-on — in the angular-momentum chapter, under “Spin Correlation Measurements and Bell's Inequality” (§3.10, with the spin-singlet correlations at §3.10.1). That is the closest the ordinary course canon comes, and it is where a reader should go to see EPR and Bell handled inside a standard course.

Result side. Nielsen & Chuang, §2.6 ("EPR and the Bell inequality", p. 111 in the 10th Anniversary Edition), where the CHSH inequality and its quantum violation are derived.

Where the paper is the story. This is one of the few essays where the originals repay reading, because their motive is the point. Einstein, Podolsky & Rosen, Phys. Rev. 47 (1935) 777 — note the actual conclusion, incompleteness, not paradox. Bell, Physics 1 (1964) 195 — four pages that turned it into a number. CHSH, Phys. Rev. Lett. 23 (1969) 880; Tsirelson's bound, Cirel'son, Lett. Math. Phys. 4 (1980) 93; Aspect et al. (1982) and the loophole-free tests of Hensen et al., Nature 526 (2015) 682.

Check the crossing. Confirm $|S|\le 2$ yourself from the bracket argument (one of $B(b)\pm B(b')$ is always zero), then substitute $E(a,b)=-\cos\theta_{ab}$ with the four angles above to recover $-2\sqrt2$. The whole theorem lives in the gap between "$\pm 2$ for every $\lambda$" and "$2\sqrt2$ for the singlet."
